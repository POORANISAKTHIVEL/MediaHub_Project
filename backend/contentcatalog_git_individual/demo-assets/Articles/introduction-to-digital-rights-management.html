Introduction to Digital Rights Management

Digital Rights Management (DRM) is the set of technologies and policies that control how licensed digital content — video, audio, images, and text — can be accessed, copied, and shared after it's been distributed.

Why DRM exists

A content owner licenses their work to a platform under specific terms: which territories it can be shown in, which devices can play it, whether downloads are allowed, and for how long. Without a technical enforcement layer, those terms are just words in a contract. DRM is what actually makes "licensed for streaming only in this region" a rule the software enforces rather than a rule someone has to trust the viewer to follow.

The building blocks

  • Encryption — the content itself is unreadable without a valid key.
  • License issuance — a server decides, per request, whether this user/device/ session is allowed to get that key right now.
  • Playback enforcement — the player checks the license's rules (expiry, device limits, output restrictions) before and during playback.

DRM in a subscription platform

For a platform like this one, DRM concerns show up as very practical questions: does this user's subscription tier include this content's access level? Is their subscription still active? Has the content been geo-restricted? Those checks are what separate "the file is on our server" from "the file is actually available to this specific viewer right now."

Common misconceptions

DRM is often confused with copyright itself — it isn't. Copyright is the legal right; DRM is the technical mechanism that makes a licensing agreement enforceable in practice. A piece of content can be copyrighted with no DRM at all, and DRM without a valid underlying license protects nothing legally.
